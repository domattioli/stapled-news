How Far Each Outlet Drifts From the Consensus Headline

Data spans —

What is STAPLE? It comes from medical imaging (Warfield et al., 2004). When several radiologists trace the same tumor and disagree, STAPLE — Simultaneous Truth and Performance Level Estimation — builds the best-estimate outline with no answer key. The trick is that it is not a simple majority vote. It starts with a rough vote, scores how reliable each radiologist is against that draft, then redraws the outline weighting the careful tracers more than the sloppy ones — and repeats until the estimate and the reliability scores stop moving. Each rater gets two numbers: sensitivity (do they catch what is really there?) and specificity (do they avoid marking what isn't?). We run the same loop on news: outlets are the radiologists, a breaking story is the tumor, headlines are the tracings. STAPLE learns which outlets phrase stories like the reliable consensus, builds the consensus headline, and measures how far each outlet drifts from it.

WHY THIS EXISTS

Measuring the Wording That Outlets Converge On

Media bias debate is loud. Usually it turns on which stories outlets choose to cover, or which side they take when they do cover them. This project measures something narrower—and checkable. We collect headlines from the same breaking news story across multiple outlets, find the exact wording they converge on, and measure how far each outlet's version drifts from that consensus wording. That drift is a standalone metric: it tells you whether an outlet phrases stories the way everyone else does, not whether it's true or whether it leans left or right.

A large share of apparent agreement is just wire syndication. When a story hits the wire, dozens of outlets run the headline verbatim. Before we measure consensus, we collapse those byte-identical copies to a single vote—otherwise syndication would manufacture a false consensus signal. The rest is honest wording distance from the pack. That's all this measures: a wording lens, not a truth or stance detector.

The method, in plain English

When a big political story breaks, every outlet writes its own headline for the same underlying facts. We collect those headlines, group them by story, and average them into a single 'consensus headline' — the wording the field converges on. Then we measure how far each outlet's version sits from that average, word by word. An outlet that phrases stories the way everyone else does scores low; an outlet that reaches for its own framing scores high. That's all 'drift' means here: distance from the pack's wording — not accuracy, and not ideology.

THE TAKE

The first thing the data shows is who hugs the consensus: regional chain papers. ocregister.com currently sits closest to the stapled headline (drift 0.04), and the rest of the nearest tier looks the same — outlets that run the wire headline nearly verbatim. That is syndication made visible, not editorial virtue. At the other end sits axios.com (drift 0.71), whose house style rewrites every headline it touches. If you came in expecting drift to be a partisanship detector, this is your first clue it isn't one: the extremes of the board are about how outlets write, not what they believe.

So is the consensus itself secretly leaning? We checked. The three blocs' average distance from consensus is statistically indistinguishable — every confidence interval overlaps. But distance is not direction. When we measure where the consensus wording sits between left-camp and right-camp language centroids, it sits left of the midpoint. Left-leaning outlets contribute about 64 percent of the coverage-weighted mass that builds the consensus, center outlets 16 percent, right outlets 20 percent. The leftward lean is mostly mechanical from that composition: the average of a left-heavy room leans left. Make of that what you will; we read it as headline language being far more standardized than the discourse about media bias suggests, and the lean itself reflecting the panel, not hidden editorial drift.

The usual caveat, stated plainly rather than buried: this corpus is 23 stories deep. The collector adds headlines every hour, the intervals will tighten, and some of what you see below will move. That's not a bug in the analysis — that's what early data is supposed to do.

DOES THE CONSENSUS LEAN?

The Direction of the Consensus, Not Just Its Distance

TL;DR  Distance treats left and right the same. This asks a sharper question: which side's wording does the consensus actually sound like? Answer: a bit left of center — because the room is mostly left.

The earlier ranking measured distance — how far each outlet's wording sits from consensus — and found the lean buckets equidistant. But distance is not direction. To test direction, for the 8 stories that had both left-rated and right-rated outlets, we build a left-camp wording centroid and a right-camp wording centroid and locate the consensus headline between them. The finding, stated plainly: the consensus sits at position 0.30 on a 0=left-camp to 1=right-camp axis (95% CI 0.21–0.39), i.e. LEFT of the 0.5 midpoint, and the interval excludes 0.5.

A panel that is 72% left-leaning produces a consensus whose wording sits left of center. That is the panel composition showing through — the consensus is an average, and the average of a left-heavy room leans left.

Caveat. Only 8 stories had both camps present (regional chain papers, which dominate the corpus, are unrated by AllSides and excluded from this axis); camp separation averages 1.01, so there genuinely is a distinguishable left-vs-right way to phrase these stories; this measures wording direction, not factual slant, and the leftward position is largely mechanical from composition rather than evidence of hidden editorial bias. As the corpus grows and more two-camp stories accrue, this estimate will firm up.
