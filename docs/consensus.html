Wire Syndication, Not Political Lean, Predicts How Far an Outlet's Headline Sits from Consensus

Data spans —

What is STAPLE? It comes from medical imaging (Warfield et al., 2004). When several radiologists trace the same tumor and disagree, STAPLE (Simultaneous Truth and Performance Level Estimation) builds the best-estimate outline with no answer key. It is not a simple majority vote: it starts with a rough vote, scores how reliable each radiologist is against that draft, then redraws the outline weighting the careful tracers more than the sloppy ones, and repeats until the estimate and the reliability scores stop moving. Each rater gets two numbers: sensitivity (do they catch what is really there?) and specificity (do they avoid marking what isn't?). We run the same loop on news: outlets are the radiologists, a breaking story is the tumor, headlines are the tracings. STAPLE learns which outlets phrase stories like the reliable consensus, builds the consensus headline, and measures how far each outlet drifts from it.

WHY THIS EXISTS

Wording Distance Measures Outlet Style, Not Editorial Stance

Media bias debate turns on story choice and stance. This project measures something narrower: whether an outlet phrases breaking news like the rest of the field or rewords it. We collect headlines from the same story across outlets, find the exact consensus wording, and measure drift: how far each outlet's version sits from that consensus. Drift is a style metric. It does not measure truth or lean. An outlet that writes like everyone else scores low; an outlet that rewrites scores high.

A large share of apparent agreement is wire syndication. When a story hits the wire, dozens of outlets run the headline verbatim. Before we measure consensus, we collapse byte-identical copies to a single vote; uncollapsed, syndication would manufacture false consensus. The rest is honest distance from the pack. Metric: wording divergence, not a truth detector and not a stance detector.

The method, in plain English

When a big political story breaks, every outlet writes its own headline for the same underlying facts. We collect those headlines, group them by story, and average them into a single 'consensus headline': the wording the field converges on. That average weights each ideological camp (left, center, right, unrated) equally, so a story covered by more left-rated than right-rated outlets does not let the larger camp define 'consensus' by outnumbering the other. We then measure how far each outlet's version sits from that average, word by word. An outlet that phrases stories the way everyone else does scores low; an outlet that reaches for its own framing scores high. That is what 'drift' means here: distance from the pack's wording, not accuracy and not ideology.

Outlets at either end of the ranking typically have few analyzed headlines each, so they mark the range rather than the most statistically stable individual estimates. The gap reflects outlet style, not political lean: outlets near consensus tend to run wire copy verbatim, while outlets far from it write more distinctive headlines for the same story. Left, center, and right outlets differ only slightly in mean drift (see the next section for the exact gap); distance is not a useful lean detector in practice.

Does the consensus wording itself lean, independent of style? A story covered by 51 percent left-rated outlet share and 24 percent right-rated share produces a raw average that leans left for arithmetic reasons alone, regardless of what any outlet actually wrote. We correct for this: the consensus centroid used for the direction analysis below weights left, center, right, and unrated outlets equally per story, so no single camp defines "consensus" simply by outnumbering the others. THE UNRATED HALF

Unrated Outlets Are Half the Corpus and Sit Closer to Consensus Than Rated Nationals

Mean drift by rating status (exact values on the bars above). Unrated outlets sit nearer the consensus yet carry no AllSides lean label, so the direction analysis in the next section cannot individually place the outlets shaping consensus most.

Limitation: unrated outlets shape the consensus without being individually placed on the left-right axis. The 491 outlets AllSides does not rate, mostly regional and international affiliates, sit closer to consensus than rated nationals (see the chart above) and are the majority contributor in most analyzed stories. The direction analysis (next section) weights an "unrated" bucket equally alongside left, center, and right when building its bias-corrected consensus centroid, so unrated wording is not invisible to that estimate. What remains impossible is placing any individual unrated outlet on the left-right axis itself: AllSides supplies no rating to project against. Expanding AllSides coverage, or adopting a secondary rating source, would let more of the corpus be individually placed rather than folded into a single unrated bucket.

WHAT'S NEXT

Five Paths: Data Depth, Method Improvement, Visualization, and Validation at Scale

  1. More two-camp stories. The direction axis draws on a narrower subset (stories with both a left-rated and a right-rated outlet) than the aggregate distance ranking; continued daily collection grows that subset and tightens its confidence interval, and should also help split-half stability (V2) clear its gate, which it does not yet on the current corpus.
  2. Expand unrated coverage. An AllSides 2024 CSV plus an MBFC fallback would encode more of the outlets the direction analysis currently folds into a single unrated bucket, letting more of the corpus be individually placed on the left-right axis.
  3. Near-duplicate syndication beyond exact matches. Current method catches byte-identical wire copy. MinHash would capture light edits ("The Latest: …", trailing attributions) that exact-match dedup misses.
  4. Embedding distance instead of lexical. Sentence embeddings measure semantic drift; two headlines paraphrasing the same idea read as closer than surface-word cosine allows.
  5. New visualizations: outlet × story heatmap and syndication UpSet. Heatmap (highest information per pixel) shows coverage gaps + drift patterns together; UpSet displays which outlet combinations republish wire copy verbatim.
